Welcome to my website! I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at the University of British Columbia. My research interests are Monetary Economics, Macroeconomics, and Time-Series Econometrics. My work focuses on the identification of macroeconomic shocks, in particular high-frequency instruments and how to combine several imperfect instruments in structural VARs. Job Market Paper

Between Weak and Contaminated: Identifying Monetary Policy Shocks with Imperfect Instruments

Presentations: Canadian Economics Association (2026); Student T2M Conference, HEC Montréal (2026); Banco de México (2026)

No available instrument for monetary policy shocks is both strong and clean. Orthogonalising high-frequency surprises on pre-announcement information makes them cleaner but weakens their first-stage strength, and that weakness magnifies any residual contamination. Instruments that cover the whole policy decision, rather than the narrow window around the announcement, are strong but exposed to other contamination. I group instruments built from the same construction into families, whose members share their contamination but differ in relevance. Demeaning within each family removes the shared contamination, and pooling across families strengthens the first stage. In U.S. data, the pooled instrument is much stronger than the orthogonalised surprise and identifies a gradual decline in output and prices and a widening of credit spreads.

Work in Progress

Necessary but Not Sufficient: Orthogonalising Monetary Policy Surprises

High-frequency monetary policy surprises are predictable from information available before the announcement. The standard remedy consists of orthogonalising them on pre-announcement controls with a single slope. I show that this remedy is necessary but not sufficient. A single slope removes the predictable part only if markets misjudge the Fed's response to news by the same amount at every announcement. The data do not support this: easing surprises are far more predictable than tightening surprises, their slopes differ, and the standard instrument remains predictable among tightening surprises. In an external-instrument SVAR, separate slopes for easing and tightening surprises keep more of the instrument's strength and more than halve the output response the standard instrument implies. What the orthogonalisation removes also depends on the choice of controls. Sets of controls that predict the surprise equally well but remove different news identify materially different responses. I extend the controls to a broad panel of pre-announcement news and summarise it with principal components that fit as well as the standard controls. The estimated effects of policy still depend on how much of the surprise the orthogonalisation removes.
