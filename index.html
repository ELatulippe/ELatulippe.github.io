Welcome to my website! I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at the University of British Columbia. My research interests are Monetary Economics, Macroeconomics, and Time-Series Econometrics. My work focuses on the identification of macroeconomic shocks, in particular high-frequency instruments and how to combine several imperfect instruments in structural VARs. Job Market Paper

When Cleaning Weakens: Pooling Instruments to Identify Monetary Policy Shocks

Previously circulated as: “High-Frequency Monetary Policy Surprises: A Cautionary Tale”

Presentations: Canadian Economics Association (2026); Student T2M Conference, HEC Montréal (2026); Banco de México (2026)

Several instruments have been proposed to measure monetary policy shocks, but none is both strong and valid. The workhorse high-frequency surprise is predictable from pre-announcement information. Orthogonalising it on that information weakens its first stage. Any residual contamination not spanned by the orthogonalising controls is then magnified by the weaker first stage, and the overall bias can be amplified. The responses it identifies are larger than those of conventional schemes, with wider bands, prices falling on impact and a reversal of the funds rate. I therefore widen the window from the announcement to the decision, and revisit instruments that cover the whole monetary policy decision rather than a narrow window around its announcement. Decision-based instruments, an extended Romer and Romer (2004) instrument and a recursive, Cholesky-based instrument, retain most of their first-stage strength after orthogonalisation on the same pre-announcement information. To raise first-stage strength further, I group the instruments into families that share a common exposure to non-policy shocks, and hence common residual contamination, but differ in relevance. Pooling within each family removes the common contamination and identifies the policy shock from the differences in relevance, and the families combine into a single pooled instrument with a stronger first stage. Applied to U.S. data, the pooled instrument is strong where the orthogonalised surprise is weak, and it identifies a standard monetary policy contraction.

Work in Progress

Purging Monetary Policy Surprises: Necessary but Not Sufficient

High-frequency monetary policy surprises are predictable from pre-announcement information, and orthogonalizing them on that information has become standard practice. I show that this purge is necessary but not sufficient, through three results. First, what the purge delivers is conditional on the control set. The raw surprise implies that output rises after a tightening; orthogonalization corrects the sign, but the magnitude remains set-identified: the peak output response varies by a factor of two across equally defensible sets of pre-announcement controls. Second, predictability is not spread evenly across announcements. I document a high-frequency footprint of the Fed put: the predictable component is an easing response to financial deterioration, so it concentrates in easing surprises, while tightenings, which typically follow ordinary good-news data, are already close to clean. A symmetric projection fitted largely on the easings therefore over-cleans the near-clean tightenings. Third, even the recommended purging order is not innocuous at meeting frequency: the pre-announcement controls are measured over multi-week windows that can overlap the previous announcement, so they have already moved in response to the previous policy shock, and purging can remove genuine monetary variation, weakening the first stage.
